# Equipment value and recoverable progress

## Measure purchases over the whole expedition

The useful incremental lesson is narrower than simply making upgrades stronger. A Meltopia reviewer described earning roughly 200 per trip while pursuing an upgrade costing nearly 5,000; another reported that more resource drops increased the number of returns rather than improving the expedition. A separate review found bag capacity could bind long before the bought fuel capacity became useful. These are individual estimates, not benchmark measurements, but they explain how an improvement on one axis can feel ineffective in play. [^234140844][^229720156][^225229511]

Super Motherload contributes a related complaint: appealing special upgrades can be priced beyond the point where the player has meaningful work left for them. Positive counterevidence complicates the diagnosis. A recommended Meltopia review still describes poor second-tool economics, while a Super Motherload positive emphasizes that collecting and combining minerals deliberately can outperform indiscriminate extraction. The latter is evidence that practical decision quality can matter; it is not a recommendation to import a combo or recipe system. [^124007291][^231984785][^212396142]

**Repository gap.** `56` already specifies meaningful matched-route benefits, and `37` already covers changing constraints, late purchases and the full run. The remaining weakness is the missing explicit measurement of the gap between an adjacent purchase, its cost in repeated trips and its realized benefit in the complete loop. A faster shovel may be delightful for shaping a chamber even when it does not raise income per minute. Conversely, a statistically faster tool should not be called a useful economic upgrade solely because a fixture loses terrain faster.

**Recommendation.** For each significant purchase, record intended benefit, money still needed, expeditions to afford it, first useful application, and subsequent dig/reveal/return/station/outbound time. Compare plausible mixed equipment states rather than only equal-level equipment. Include the case where faster extraction fills the bag sooner and the case where more capacity is limited by remaining return energy. Use these observations to tune the existing tracks, not force all constraints to expire simultaneously.

Track long stretches with neither a wanted purchase nor a new recognizable discovery. A single target such as the last important purchase at 75–85% of completion cannot expose an empty middle. Contrast an unlucky ordinary-income route, an early lucky valuable find and a purchase order favoring movement or capacity. If a claimed milestone does not change the task it promises to help, revise its cost or effect; seek review if that requires shortening a selected track. Do not lengthen the world to justify an upgrade count.

## Protect a finite economy from permanent loss

One Meltopia reviewer reported being unable to reach profitable areas after a balance change while lacking money for the necessary warmth upgrade. A Super Motherload account describes arriving at a late challenge without the required bombs and being unable to buy them locally. These reports establish failure patterns involving access, money and required equipment; they do not demonstrate an identical flaw in Something Down There. [^226130070][^214946233]

**Repository evidence.** This project has a more specific exposure: generated finds are finite, collection identities persist, ordinary carried items are lost on rescue, and those lost finds do not respawn. The runtime's `RescueController.TryConfirm` removes the quoted inventory; `BuriedFind` retains its collected state. The current zero-wallet rescue remains available, but returning safely to the surface is different from retaining a financially viable route through the future campaign. Tasks `59` and `50` mention avoiding softlocks without defining the remaining-income test, while `37` mostly compares representative successful runs.

**Recommendation.** In `56`, identify whether any ordinary paid capability is actually necessary to finish, as opposed to simply faster or more comfortable. In `59`, state the supported recovery guarantee: what must remain possible after ordinary mistakes, which losses are irreversible, and what optional all-upgrade completion can reasonably promise. The desired outcome is a finishable save with understandable consequences. Finite resources, arbitrary repeated loss and guaranteed purchase of every optional upgrade cannot all be assumed without an explicit additional policy.

Test a low wallet after losing a high-value ordinary bag, several early rescues, investment in nonessential tracks before a necessary capability, and an extensively depleted shallow region. For each state, calculate accessible unsold value against remaining necessary costs and demonstrate the route using normal equipment. An income total is insufficient if that income sits behind the upgrade being funded. Include any consumable cost only if that system is eventually selected.

If the future game cannot meet its existing promise that rescue will not make finishing impossible, bring a concrete remedy back to the owning design task. Adjusting cost bands or ensuring the normal kit can finish may suffice; guaranteed replacement loot, refunds, infinite money and emergency power are not selected by this audit. Preserve the finite saved population and the existing rescue decision until a different policy is reviewed. Task `37` must report the recovery scenarios separately from its ordinary 2–3-hour pacing runs.

## Validate recovery, not only spawn validity

A positive Meltopia review describes loot left high on cave ceilings where ordinary collection became awkward and proposed improved explosive placement. The Super Motherload account above also describes overlapping areas that could not be reached with its available bomb-placement rules. These are useful examples of the distinction between an object existing in a valid location and an ordinary player having a practical way to recover it. Meltopia's developer later announced fixes for dropped artifacts and out-of-bounds handling; this audit therefore treats such reports as historical design cases rather than a verified current defect list. [^224608018][^214946233][^ice-fixes]

**Repository gap.** `45` already rejects invalid overlaps, boundary violations and poor novelty distribution. `47` already tests controlled traversal. Neither explicitly joins those contracts at a collectible's actual interaction point after the surrounding excavation has changed. `BuriedFind.TryCollect` checks an actual collider hit within interaction reach; a visible object or detector signal alone does not make it collectible. The current development shapes cannot establish recovery for the full authored roster.

**Recommendation.** Add representative ceiling, high wall, oblique cluster, cramped recess and near-boundary cases using authored object bounds and actual exposure samples. Widen or remove nearby ground, leave with a full bag, return, and reload before collecting. Verify that objects remain obtainable with ordinary bought reach and flight; a required find must not depend on dynamite, developer teleportation or an unselected construction tool. This should test the existing excavation and jetpack capabilities, not add an automatic vacuum or a new rescue mechanic for objects.

Use deterministic placement checks for conditions they can establish, followed by practical recovery in a small recorded set of adversarial shapes. Infinite testing of every player excavation is not realistic, but a release that has never tried an exposed ceiling find is avoidably weak. Preserve collected IDs and deliberate route freedom throughout; the remedy for an impossible case must not quietly reroll the player's world.
