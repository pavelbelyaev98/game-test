# Discovery identity and payoff

## Make a discovery valuable as an experience

Meltopia provides the clearest warning: a collectible can have an elaborate presentation and still disappoint. One reviewer expected artifacts to affect progression and found their display-only role unrewarding; another kept collecting sets in anticipation of an outcome that never arrived. A third enjoyed the upgrade animations and snow removal but wanted more context for the artifacts. These accounts concern a mismatch between the effort, presentation and result, not proof that every collectible needs a stat bonus. [^225520385][^226191402][^225106238]

Positive counterevidence matters. Another Meltopia reviewer explicitly enjoyed the relics and bones alongside a simple, generally low-stress loop. A Game About Digging A Hole also has players who value the excavation itself and are content with a modest story wrapper. A mandatory buff, quest or description for every find would misread that audience. [^224698768][^216693861]

**Repository gap.** Task `40` already requires identity, shape, novelty groups, clusters and optional flavor. Task `89` is unusually precise about starter quantities and visual specifications. Neither yet asks each proposed find to state the payoff it promises and how the actual reward fulfills that promise. Because the detector intentionally foregrounds physical significance rather than price, an imposing signal can lead to a cheap object; that is defensible, but the physical discovery must carry enough interest to make the encounter worthwhile. More money is only one possible reward.

**Recommendation.** Add a compact recognition/reward rationale to the existing roster entries. For a routine bottle, recognition and useful incidental income may suffice. A recognizable mechanical fragment could invite a lateral investigation; an incongruous household object might earn a laugh; a distinctive object may become a meaningful personal photograph. These are examples of roles within the existing design, not selections of new objects. Record whether an entry promises only a collectible, an actual equipment benefit, or an ending function, and avoid presentation that suggests an unimplemented reward.

For related clusters, define a recognizable relationship rather than merely spawning several models near one another. After the first part is found, the player should be able to form a plausible hypothesis about the surrounding excavation without a quest arrow or a required text explanation. Review the full assembled scene and the partly uncovered encounter, not only isolated catalogue renders. The question is whether the composition makes curiosity reasonable; it need not guarantee a jackpot or insist that every player follows it.

## Demonstrate the appeal in the opening

Several negative Hole reviews describe too little to discover beyond sellable ores, while one points out that apparently special treasure arrived too late to be useful. A positive reviewer, however, was happy to finish a careful excavation in a little over an hour and replay briefly. The useful distinction is perceived substance during the experience, rather than the largest possible content count. [^219815272][^206740959][^212496660]

**Repository gap.** The first production trip, `15`, checks a full mechanical loop and already includes an unguided observation. Full distinctive content arrives later in `42`–`44`. Without an explicit content-learning question, it is possible to pass the trip while proving little about the game's main attraction beyond buying a faster shovel. Ordinary starter objects can establish recognition and character; this does not require moving late mystery content into the opening.

**Recommendation.** In `89`, explain how the proposed starter batch earns attention through silhouettes, condition of burial, juxtaposition or grounded humor. Use the quantities already being designed; do not inflate the batch simply to meet a research checklist. In `09` and `15`, observe the first recognizable discovery, first unexpected or personally interesting moment, and first purchase the player wants. Ask what they remember and what they want to find next before explaining the intended interpretation. A technically successful trip that leaves the player remembering only money and dirt is a reason to revise the batch or presentation before approving the full roster.

Compare a small, recorded set of accepted starter seeds and legitimate downward/lateral approaches. This should expose an opening that depends on a single fortunate placement without imposing a scripted find at a prescribed minute. Time to recognition and the player's explanation are evidence, not numerical guarantees. Keep the 40% exposure, immediate eligible pickup and current input rules unless actual observation supports a separately reviewed change.

## Make the personal photograph retain the reward

Some Meltopia players found artifacts unmotivating because they became static display pieces without context. That does not invalidate Something Down There's display, but it raises its burden: the photograph needs to recall a discovery the player cared about. One positive Hole review describes planning an elegant spiral excavation and valuing the act of making it, which supports preserving the personal setting of a find rather than replacing it with a stock image. [^225520385][^216693861]

**Repository gap.** `60` already owns capture timing and `51` owns durable images and display browsing. The missing criterion is whether the actual captured image is legible and worth revisiting. At the accepted 40% threshold, a player can collect from an oblique view with much of the object still hidden. A technically correct first-frame capture could preserve mostly dirt, a tool silhouette or a tiny object in darkness. This is a predicted integration risk; the snapshot feature is not yet implemented.

**Recommendation.** Have `60` compare concrete framing and capture-timing proposals using the player's actual excavation. Evaluate partial burial, unusual orientations, rapid held pickup, cramped spaces and the chosen underground light. A future capture solution must preserve immediate collection and must not manufacture a falsely clean excavation. Decide how to handle a poor capture in that design task rather than silently adding recapture controls, delaying pickup or overwriting the first photo later.

For `51`, include player recognition at the display's actual viewing size, several adjacent similar-looking finds, the full intended photo count, and persistence after selling and relaunch. The useful acceptance question is whether a player can identify the object and remember something about finding it, not merely whether a PNG exists. Keep captions at name plus depth, with no new counters or mandatory reading.
