# Something Down There — pitch

**Genre:** first-person excavation and discovery  
**Platform:** Windows PC, keyboard and mouse  
**Length:** about 2–3 hours for a first completion; randomized sites for repeat runs  
**Tone:** bright, funny, escalating absurdity

## Logline

You arrive at a drained reservoir with a shovel and a business plan: get rich digging up whatever the water left behind. You dig, the detector whispers that something is nearby, and you pull out junk, coins, machinery, bones — and things that should not be there. You sell it all to buy a slightly more unreasonable digging machine. The hole gets deeper, the equipment gets stranger, and the last discovery changes what this place is.

## Why it works

- **The next object may surprise you.** Every cut can reveal something funny, valuable or quietly wrong; digging and discovery carry the whole game.
- **Your hole is the reward.** Digging is shaping. Routes, shelters, shortcuts and the look of the excavation belong to the player.
- **Visible, absurd escalation.** A basic shovel becomes modified, powered, motorized and finally an unreasonable homemade machine. Upgrades change what the tool can do, not only how fast.
- **A calm game with a growing mystery.** It starts believable and ends impossible — without switching genres to get there.

## The loop

Dig → the detector hints at something nearby → investigate and expose it → collect → decide whether to push deeper or return → sell and refill → upgrade → dig again.

The surface is a short, satisfying checkpoint: sell, refill, buy, go straight back down. Not a second game of menus, sorting and errands.

## The site

A finite, completely untouched drained reservoir. New games randomize its population: ordinary rocks, familiar household litter, and mineral bands running from coal near the surface down through copper, iron, silver, gold, emerald and ruby to diamond. The excavation is saved, so the hole persists and grows across sessions. Dig down, sideways or diagonally — nothing is pre-dug and no route is compulsory.

## Finds and the detector

- Common finds — bottles, plain rocks, everyday debris — are reliable income and always detector-silent.
- A passive detector pulses only for noteworthy finds. It never names the object, reveals its value or points a route: it invites investigation, not checklist completion.
- Finds are uncovered and collected physically. Expose enough of an object, then pick it up; optional lift, drop and throw keep it a real object in the world. No brushing minigames, appraisal timers or inventory bureaucracy.
- Rare wrong versions of familiar objects make late digging strange rather than merely richer.

## Progression

Money buys capability:
- an evolving shovel with distinct, visibly different tiers and a final absurd homemade endpoint;
- shared fuel for digging and the jetpack, refilled explicitly at the surface;
- a backpack that carries more finds per trip;
- a detector that grows from vague proximity into useful spatial information;
- rare buried permanent upgrades that reward exploration.

Every major purchase should let the player do something they could not do before, and terrain that once blocked them should become easy.

## Look and sound

Bright cartoon realism: vivid greens over warm earth, readable silhouettes and clear authored detail, under a sunny sky with short moving grass and readable underground depth. The humour is visual, physical and economic; some discoveries are genuinely cool without being a gag.

Audio direction: ambient nature — wind, birds, insects, weather — plus tactile feedback for digging, footsteps, the jetpack and collecting. No music and no voice acting.

## The payoff

The game deliberately begins believable and grows absurd. One or two mid-game finds are suspicious; later, several simply do not fit; the final discovery confirms something impossible and makes the protagonist famous. The ending uses the equipment the player earned, never strips upgrades or switches to stealth, combat or puzzles, and “Continue Playing” returns to the same excavation with remaining finds and goals.

## Audience

Players who enjoy tactile digging games, clear physical progression and the “one more scoop” pull — the audience for A Game About Digging a Hole, Keep Digging and similar excavation loops — but who want stronger comedy escalation and a mystery payoff.

## What this game is not

No survival meters, crafting chains, combat, stealth, puzzles, museum management or infinite procedural world. Pressure comes from fuel, terrain and the decision to turn back. The player always does the digging; upgrades make that work more satisfying, never automated.

## Status

A playable Windows build exists today: excavation and terrain, detector, find collection, economy, saves, menus and the accepted art direction. The discovery roster, deeper progression and the ending are in active design.
