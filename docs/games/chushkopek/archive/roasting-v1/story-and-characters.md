# Story, characters, and endings

[Design index](readme.md) · original narrative proposals

## Recommended plot: Just a Few Peppers

Grandpa asks the adult player character to help prepare some peppers. He says there is a thank-you waiting in the cellar. On arrival, a couple of crates sit beside a table under the vine. The roaster is already warming outside. The family list reveals that today's work has several batches, despite his casual description.

The story is about discovering the scale of his generosity and becoming good at a familiar task. He has promised food to the aunt, someone returning to the city, and an older neighbor. He knows what he wants to give people; he is less convincing about how much work that entails.

The player initially helps with a chore, then takes charge of the workflow. By the final batch, Grandpa asks the player whether a pepper is ready. That reversal communicates competence more effectively than a large level number.

| What's in the Cellar? | Cheap anticipation from a visible locked door. | A tiny bottle alone may feel like an insulting payoff. | Secondary curiosity with a broader earned ending. |
| Neighborhood Pepper Championship | Clear comparisons and presentation challenges. | Judging systems and timed rivalry displace the calm family mood. | Optional job framing only. |

## Five story beats

**Arrival.** Grandpa offers a chair and explains the first action accurately. His quantity estimate is the joke. The player prepares lunch, sees that blackened skin can hide good food, and gets the first small shared payoff.

**The machine under the cloth.** After the first jar job, Grandpa reveals the triple roaster that has been visible since arrival. He treats it like a precious engineering achievement. The player immediately experiences the capacity improvement on the next accepted batch.

**The recipients.** Crate notes reveal who the jars are for. A relative wants neat peppers for a meal; another needs food packed to take home. These details explain the different job specifications. They do not require new NPCs to walk into the scene.

**The recipe.** A short recipe card introduces the lyutenitsa batch. Grandpa supplies the prepared tomato base and one opinion about consistency. If that branch is cut, this beat becomes the particularly neat family-gift batch instead; the emotional arc survives.

**The real last crate.** The board identifies the final main job. The player completes it with the tools already earned. The camera remains under player control as the empty working area and finished food become visible together. Grandpa acknowledges the effort and gives the cellar key.

## Small cast, clear roles

| Character | Role in play | Affordable presentation | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grandpa | Companion, first practical lesson, optional fixed-station peeling help. | One seated character, a few short authored gestures, subtitles, limited recorded lines if feasible. | Unreliable critical instructions, repeated shouting, wandering helper AI. |
| Aunt | Careful jar presentation and family recipient notes. | Handwritten cards and occasional offscreen lines. | Turning her into a permanent scolding timer. |
| Neighbor at the fence | Optional favors, competing opinions, returned containers. | Voice from a fixed direction or one brief silhouette. | A simulated neighborhood, schedules, pathfinding. |
| Player's family member | Gives the final packed box a destination. | A postcard/photo and short ending message. | A branching relationship system. |

Grandpa can have a glass and a bottle nearby, as the user suggested. They are background character details and an optional final toast. Do not make repeated drinking a required stat boost, introduce intoxicated camera movement, or grow a second rakia-production game here.

## Dialogue delivery

Main instructions always appear in clear job cards and action prompts. Banter is optional, subtitled, and quieter than roast cues. First-time explanations pause the work; idle remarks do not take camera control.

Budget roughly **24–30 short lines total**, including the candidates in [Jobs, events, and comedy](jobs-events-and-comedy.md). Script important lines once. Use a long cooldown for incidental lines, and allow banter to be reduced or disabled. The game should remain understandable and satisfying with dialogue muted.

Keep the tone affectionate and specific: mismatched lids, a respected old tool, wildly optimistic quantities, family pride. Avoid generic “everyone is drunk and incompetent” jokes. Grandpa should occasionally be the person who knows something useful that the player has just learned to appreciate.

## Ending that pays off the work

The ending has three rewards, in this order:

1. **The player's result:** finished shelves and parcels, with a clear view back toward the empty crate area.
2. **Recognition:** Grandpa trusts the player's judgment and gives them the family recipe card or the old single-slot roaster as a keepsake.
3. **The cellar joke:** the promised thank-you is a carefully wrapped family package, including a small bottle labeled “For special occasions.” Under it is a place already reserved for the player's jars.

The comedy should add to the first two rewards. Do not tease treasure for an hour and then deliver only a cheap bottle. The player keeps their agency, can inspect the pantry, and can take a campaign photograph before leaving.

Suggested final exchange, original draft:

> Grandpa: „Видя ли? Само няколко.“ — “See? Just a few.”
>
> The player looks at the full shelves.
>
> Grandpa: „Добре де. Няколко рафта.“ — “All right. A few shelves.”

End credits after the player chooses to finish. An optional “Another small favor” board appears afterward; it is not a twist that postpones the ending.
